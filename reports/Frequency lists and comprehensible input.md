# Let frequency pick the next word

The video is mostly right, with two corrections it probably left out. The research strongly supports deliberately learning the most common words first. Flashcard-style study is among the most efficient ways to learn vocabulary, and the first two or three thousand words carry most of everyday speech. The payoff per word is steepest at George's stage, a few hundred words in. The research equally supports large amounts of input he can mostly understand. It does *not* support three things the online version often adds: that input alone is enough, that there is a magic "98% known" threshold, and that he should stay silent for hundreds of hours before speaking. The mainstream position is a balanced mix of input, speaking, deliberate study and fluency practice.

For Hebrew there is a counting problem. Raw frequency lists count spellings, and because the Hebrew for "the", "in", "and" and "to" is written joined to the next word, those lists scramble the ranks. In one open subtitle list the bare word *ir* ("city") ranks 1,998th while *ha'ir* ("the city") ranks 477th. Any list the app uses has to be counted by dictionary word (the lemma) and then cleaned by hand.

The Hebrew Reader has never used a frequency list, and no design document mentions comprehensible input. Even so, it already enforces a stricter version of the "understand almost everything" rule than the research asks for on every practice sentence. What it lacks is any sense of which unknown word matters most. New words arrive in Duolingo's order and by how often grandad says them, about two per session, so the choice of the next word is where a frequency list has the most leverage.

## Frequency-first study pays most at 500 words, and stops paying at about 3,000

**Why common words matter so much.** The best-known coverage figures come from Paul Nation's analysis of English. The **first 1,000 word families account for about 81–84% of the words in speech**. The second 1,000 adds only 5–6 points in speech (8–9 in writing). The third adds 2–3. From about the sixth thousand onward, each extra thousand adds under one point ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)). In unscripted conversation, **3,000 families plus names cover over 95% of the words spoken** ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)). TV and film are similar: 95% of the words at 2,000–3,000 families, 98% at 4,000–5,000 ([Ha 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8899723)).

The practical upshot for someone around 500 words is that the next 1,000–2,000 common words are the richest stretch of the whole curve. Each thousand adds more coverage than every band past 5,000 put together.

**Studying words on purpose works.** A meta-analysis of 22 studies found that deliberate word-learning activities produced **average gains of about 60% on immediate meaning-recall tests**. Flashcards and word lists gave "relatively large gains", while writing exercises and fill-in-the-blanks gave relatively small ones ([Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12671)).

**Flashcard words become real vocabulary.** A common worry is that card-learned words stay "facts" rather than becoming usable vocabulary. In a lab study, words learned from flashcards over one week went on to behave like real vocabulary in automatic processing tests ([Elgort 2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00613.x)).

**Picking words up from input alone is slower.** How many times a learner meets a word correlates only moderately with learning it (**r = .34** across 26 studies) ([Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343)). That is the case for pairing the two approaches rather than choosing one.

**Where general frequency stops helping.** The researchers who argue hardest for frequency also mark where it stops. Schmitt & Schmitt now define "high-frequency" vocabulary as the top 3,000 word families. They treat 3,000–9,000 as a "mid-frequency" band that learners need, but which is too large and too thinly spread to march through in rank order ([Schmitt & Schmitt 2014](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1040866)). Nation's figures show why. In a whole novel, most of the sixth-thousand words appear exactly once. In the film *Shrek*, the story's own recurring words (ogre, swamp, donkey) sit far down the general list, yet they behave like known words once learned ([Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)).

So past about 3,000 words, the right question stops being "what is common in the language" and becomes "what is common in *my* input". For George, that input is grandad's voice notes.

**There is no magic threshold.** The famous "you must know 98% of the words" figure was a statistical extrapolation from 66 learners; nobody in that study was actually tested at 98%. The best evidence since then shows comprehension rising in a **straight line with coverage, with no point where it suddenly clicks**. Readers understood about half of a text when they knew 90% of its words, and about 75% even when they knew every word ([Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)). A pre-registered replication in 2023 confirmed the straight line and **failed to replicate the 98% threshold** ([Kremmel et al. 2023](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724)).

Listening seems more forgiving. Mean comprehension was already about 73% at 90% coverage ([van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/34/4/457/199564)). Treat 95% and 98% as useful targets, not gates.

## Comprehensible input is necessary, and the field stopped calling it sufficient

**What Krashen claimed, and what the field kept.** Nobody serious disputes that learners need a great deal of input they can mostly understand. What the field has dropped are Krashen's stronger claims: that input is *all* you need, and that consciously studied knowledge can never become real acquisition. Those claims were challenged on three fronts:

- **Swain** found that Canadian immersion students with years of input still lacked accuracy. She argued that learners need "pushed output": trying to say things, which is how they notice the gaps ([JALT review of Swain](https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2198-output-and-beyond-dialogue-review-merrill-swains-current-approach-sla)).
- **Long** showed that the back-and-forth of making yourself understood drives development ([Cambridge, Interaction and instructed SLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/interaction-and-instructed-second-language-acquisition/78A156EE200F744F5978F99BFB073DBE)).
- **An updated meta-analysis** found that explicit instruction outperformed implicit instruction ([Goo et al. 2015](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo)).

The frequency-deck part of the video's advice only makes sense if studied words *can* feed real acquisition. The evidence above says they can.

**The mainstream alternative: Nation's Four Strands.** The most-cited working framework gives roughly **equal time to four strands** ([Nation 2007](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2167/illt039.0)):

1. Listening and reading for meaning.
2. Speaking and writing to communicate.
3. Deliberate study of words and forms.
4. Fluency practice with already-familiar material.

"Frequency list plus comprehensible input" is strands 3 and 1. Pure comprehensible-input programmes are strand 1 turned up to nearly 100%.

**How the online recipe compares.** The recipe the video probably described is Refold's. Its steps are:

1. Learn roughly the top 1,500 words from a frequency deck.
2. Switch to heavy input.
3. From then on, "sentence-mine": save sentences from your own input that contain exactly one unknown word ([Refold vocabulary](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-1/c/vocabulary/); [Refold sentence mining](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/sentence-mining)).

Dreaming Spanish sits at the pure end. It uses no flashcards and does not recommend speaking at its first levels ([Dreaming Spanish roadmap](https://d3usdtf030spqd.cloudfront.net/Language_Learning_Roadmap_by_Dreaming_Spanish.pdf)). Its own estimates say to **double the hours for a language unrelated to yours**, which for an English speaker learning Hebrew puts "follows topics adapted for learners" (about 1,500 words) at around 300 hours of listening. Those are company estimates, not research.

The table below separates what the research supports from what is community lore.

| What the video circuit says | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| Learn the top 1,000–2,000 words first | **Supported.** Deliberate study is efficient and the top words carry most of speech. But 2,000 words still leaves learners needing adapted input, not native TV. |
| "1,000 words is 70% of any language" | Roughly true for English. "Any language" is unsourced, and in Hebrew the figure depends on how you count a word (next section). |
| "Understand 70–90% and context fills the rest" | That is the share of *meaning* understood, not the share of *words known*. The research figures (95–98% of words) measure something different. |
| "Don't speak for hundreds of hours" | **Not supported.** No controlled evidence shows early speaking harms adults, and output theory argues the opposite. |
| "Exactly one unknown word per sentence" | A sensible practical rule. It has never been tested head-to-head against two unknown words. |

**Speaking practice checked by speech recognition.** This is the core of George's practice, and it has decent support as one strand. A meta-analysis of speech-recognition pronunciation training found an overall **medium effect (g = 0.69)**, rising to **g = 0.86 when feedback said explicitly what was wrong**. The effect was large on individual sounds and small on intonation. Short trials showed no benefit, and practising alone had a smaller effect than practising with others ([Ngo, Chen & Lai, ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)). That evidence is all from learners of English. Transfer to Hebrew is an assumption.

## Hebrew scrambles raw frequency lists: *ir* ranks 1,998th until you count *ha'ir*

**Why raw Hebrew lists mislead.** The coverage figures above count English word families. None of them transfer to Hebrew as numbers, because Hebrew glues the article, "and", "in", "to", "from", "that" and "like" onto the next word. A list that counts written forms splits one word across many spellings. In the Hebrew OpenSubtitles list, the bare form *ir* is only **9% of the word's actual uses**. *Bayit* ("house") bare is 24%, and *babayit* ("in the house") out-ranks it. *Kheder* ("room") bare is 26%, below *bakheder*. **2,212 of the top 5,000 written forms are just a prefix attached to another listed form** ([Hermit Dave FrequencyWords, measured](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords/master/content/2018/he/he_full.txt)).

**Why you can't just strip the prefixes.** Merging spellings by stripping prefixes fails too, because unpointed Hebrew is full of look-alikes:

- The written form translated *mispar* ("number") is also *mesaper* ("tells").
- The form for *okhel* ("food, eats") is also *ukhal* ("I will be able").

About 23% of Hebrew words are ambiguous out of context ([Abu-Rabiah 2025](https://journals.us.edu.pl/index.php/TAPSLA/article/download/16594/14696/101753)). The only reliable fix is to analyse the words *in running sentences*, using a tool that splits off the prefixes and identifies the dictionary word. That is what Dicta's tools, HebPipe and Stanza do ([Abu-Rabiah 2025](https://journals.us.edu.pl/index.php/TAPSLA/article/download/16594/14696/101753); [HebPipe](https://github.com/amir-zeldes/HebPipe)).

**The best free list, and its flaws.** The one open, ready-made list counted by dictionary word is Juan Pinto's **Frequency Dictionary of Spoken Hebrew**. It gives the top 5,000 dictionary words from film subtitles, is MIT-licensed, and is only 157 KB ([Pinto, GitHub](https://github.com/juandpinto/frequency-dictionary)). It also has clear errors that must be fixed by hand ([FDOSH TSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/juandpinto/frequency-dictionary/master/export/frequency-dictionary.tsv)):

- All the personal pronouns collapse into *hu* ("he").
- The glued-on single letters are listed as words in their own right.
- *Beseder* ("OK") is split, so *seder* ranks 27th.
- *Hakol* ("everything") appears as the verb "to contain".

**Other lists.** The larger, better written-Hebrew list (heTenTen21, 3.1 billion words) sits behind a Sketch Engine subscription ([Sketch Engine](https://www.sketchengine.eu/hetenten-hebrew-corpus/)). No ulpan or Ministry of Education core list could be found online.

**Subtitles are the right source, with a known skew.** Subtitle frequencies predict how quickly native speakers recognise words better than book or newspaper counts do. This has been shown in English, Chinese, Dutch and Catalan ([van Heuven et al. 2014](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17470218.2013.850521); [Cai & Brysbaert 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0010729)), though nobody has tested it for Hebrew.

Hebrew subtitles are mostly translated American film and TV, so they over-rank dramatic words. *Elohim* ("God") is 101st, *met* ("dead") 99th and *kadima* ("come on") 116th ([FDOSH TSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/juandpinto/frequency-dictionary/master/export/frequency-dictionary.tsv)).

The two kinds of list do agree at the top: **91% of the written list's top 1,000 appear in the subtitle list's top 5,000** ([Abu-Rabiah 2025](https://journals.us.edu.pl/index.php/TAPSLA/article/download/16594/14696/101753)). So cleaning the list matters more than which list you pick.

**How much Hebrew is enough: nobody has measured it.** There is no Hebrew study of how many words it takes to understand 95% of speech. Measured on the subtitle list, the top 1,000 *written forms* cover only 64.7% of the text and the top 10,000 cover 85.6% ([Hermit Dave, measured](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords/master/content/2018/he/he_full.txt)). The top 1,000 *dictionary words* in Pinto's list cover 80.9%, but that is flattered because the glued-on letters count as about 15% of all words ([FDOSH, measured](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/juandpinto/frequency-dictionary/master/export/frequency-dictionary.tsv)).

A fair inference is that 2,000–3,000 dictionary words, with the prefixes known as grammar, cover somewhere around 85–90% of subtitle Hebrew. That figure is not published anywhere.

**Progress is slow even with teaching.** Arabic-speaking students who had finished **more than 1,000 hours of Hebrew instruction used only about 1,023 different words** across their university-entrance essays ([Abu-Rabiah 2025](https://journals.us.edu.pl/index.php/TAPSLA/article/download/16594/14696/101753)). The Hebrew Reader measures words George can *say* correctly, which is the harder standard.

## The app already runs a strict input gate, but has no idea which words are common

**What the app does now.** From George's side, practice works like this. Every sentence he is asked to say is built only from words he is already "ready" on, meaning he has met them and said them correctly at least once. Grammar words such as *ani*, *yesh* and *shel* are treated as known. In daily practice, one not-yet-known word may ride along, but only if every other word in the sentence is well established. Inside a map node the limit is two, and never two brand-new words ([hebrew-reader.html](../hebrew-reader.html)).

That is the online "exactly one unknown word" rule, arrived at independently. Relaxing the rule was proposed twice and turned down both times ([2026-09-02 spec](../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-02-the-writer-has-123-words-design.md); [2026-09-23 spec](../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-23-one-list-and-a-bank-that-covers-it-design.md)). The rule came from George, not from theory: it was adopted on 2026-08-11 after he said the sentences were "wayy too hard" ([2026-08-11 spec](../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-11-difficulty-and-readiness-design.md)).

This near-100%-known standard is *stricter* than the research asks of input, and that is fine. Saying a sentence aloud is fluency and output practice, and Nation's fluency strand is defined as using familiar material only.

**The app has no general frequency ranking.** Nothing in the code or the specs mentions frequency lists, Krashen or comprehensible input. The one exception is the original design note: it describes the offline dictionary as roughly 400 "high-frequency conversational words" chosen by hand ([2026-07-22 spec](../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-07-22-hebrew-reader-design.md)).

**Where George's words come from.** Words reach his study list from five sources:

1. His original spreadsheet (77 words).
2. A hand-picked core of 38 useful verbs and nouns.
3. Two lists from his Duolingo course: an early batch of 84 guessed from unit topics, and a later import that adds each unit's words once he reaches that unit.
4. Words he approves from grandad's voice notes.
5. Words the map adds for each node.

**How new words enter practice.** New words arrive as word cards: **two per full session, one per quick session**. One correct meeting makes a word ready for sentences next time.

**How the next new word is chosen.** The pick is ranked by a "familiarity" score. That score favours his own spreadsheet, early Duolingo units, how often grandad has said the word, how long it has been on the list, and past drill results ([hebrew-reader.html](../hebrew-reader.html)).

**Where Duolingo order stops tracking frequency.** Duolingo's order is a fair stand-in for frequency in the early units, but it becomes topical after about unit 20. Unit 50, "Languages", actually holds rescue and emergency words. The familiarity boost for Duolingo units also fades to zero by unit 31 ([code audit](../hebrew-reader.html)). So Duolingo order stops tracking frequency at about the point George has now reached.

**Why the next pick matters.** The app deliberately did not import all 2,178 Duolingo headwords. The spec said: "2,178 headwords against a 2-per-day introduction rate is three years of queue" ([2026-09-02 spec](../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-02-the-writer-has-123-words-design.md)). At two new words a session, **every thousand new words costs about 500 sessions**. With so few slots, which word fills each one decides how fast his coverage grows.

**Grandad's voice notes are a personal frequency list.** The count of how often grandad says each word already acts as a frequency list for George's own world. The map planner is even briefed with "words his grandad actually uses that he still cannot say" ([hebrew-reader.html](../hebrew-reader.html)). By Schmitt & Schmitt's logic, that personal count is the *better* guide beyond about 3,000 words. A general list is the scaffolding for getting there.

**Grandad's notes are also the app's closest thing to comprehensible input.** When he opens a voice note, each word appears as a card coloured by how well he knows it. The app never adds these up into a "you know X% of this note" figure ([hebrew-reader.html](../hebrew-reader.html)).

**Mapped against the four strands:**

- **Deliberate study and fluency are strong.** Word cards and spaced review cover deliberate study, and saying familiar sentences at speed covers fluency.
- **Output is present but controlled.** Whether the prompt is English or Hebrew, he says a sentence someone else composed.
- **Listening for meaning is the thinnest strand.** Of 770 items in the current content batch, 48 are listening items ([content/nodes.json](../content/nodes.json)).

## Six changes, in order of payoff

The first change is a prerequisite for the next two. The rest can go in any order. "Evidence" in the table rates how well the underlying research supports the idea, not whether the particular feature has been tested; none of these features has been tested in a controlled study.

| # | Change | What George would notice | Evidence | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build a cleaned Hebrew frequency ranking | Nothing directly: it is the foundation | Strong for the idea; the list quality needs work | Offline, one-off |
| 2 | Let the ranking choose the next new word | New word cards become the most useful missing words | Strong | Small |
| 3 | Show "% known" on voice notes, plus a top-1,000 progress count | A number on each voice note and a progress figure | Moderate (the underlying relationship is linear) | Small |
| 4 | Introduce common new words inside easy sentences | A new word's first sentence arrives surrounded by words he knows well | Plausible, untested head-to-head | Small |
| 5 | Short connected listening passages per node | A small story or dialogue to listen to before drilling a node | Strong for input; the format is design judgement | Medium |
| 6 | Use frequency to weight content writing and chunks | Common words turn up in more sentences, with more variety | Moderate | Small per batch |

**1. Build the ranking properly, once.** Start from Pinto's 5,000-word spoken list (MIT licence, so it can be bundled freely) and fix its known faults:

- Split the pronouns back out.
- Drop the single-letter rows.
- Restore *beseder* and *hakol*.
- Fold stray verb forms into their dictionary word.
- Drop the ~63 grammar words the app already treats as known.

Then match it against the app's existing dictionaries (2,630 Hebrew words with glosses). Verbs need care. The app files some verbs under the present tense (*rotse*, *holekh*) and Duolingo lists both forms separately (*holekh* in unit 16, *lalekhet* in unit 2), so the ranking should attach to whichever form the app actually uses.

Two more fixes make it trustworthy. First, hand-trim the dramatic film words (*elohim*, *met*). Second, have George's girlfriend spot-check a small random sample, not the whole list, as a register sanity check. Register matters because written Hebrew over-uses formal words such as *asher* ("which"), while subtitles lean the other way ([Ravid et al.](https://benjamins.com/catalog/ml.11.3.04rav)).

If the cleaned list still looks shaky, the stronger upgrade is to count words fresh from real subtitle sentences using DictaBERT-parse (CC BY 4.0) ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/dicta-il/dictabert-parse)). Do *not* try to convert the larger raw word list into dictionary words: without sentences, the look-alike problem cannot be solved.

**2. Let the ranking choose the next new word, but not the reviews.** Today's familiarity score is where the Duolingo unit order was folded in, and a frequency term fits the same slot. Four changes would do it:

- **Use the ranking where Duolingo stops tracking it.** Above about unit 20, frequency should replace Duolingo order as the tie-breaker.
- **Keep grandad's count as the stronger signal.** A word he actually hears is worth more than a general rank.
- **Order stretch words by the ranking.** These are the two words per map node borrowed from Duolingo units he hasn't reached, and they should be ordered by frequency instead of unit number.
- **Brief the map planner.** Tell it which common words he still lacks, the same way it is told what grandad says.

When the pool of candidate new words runs thin, the app could add the most common words he doesn't have yet, each with its existing gloss, the way map nodes already add their stretch words.

Frequency should **not** reorder reviews. The same score currently also orders due words, so this needs an explicit decision when it is built. Due-ness should keep deciding reviews. A small extra: the Duolingo words that were imported *assumed known* and are being "tested gently" could be tested most-common first.

**3. Put a coverage number on each voice note.** The colours are already computed. Summing them into "you know 88% of the words in this note" costs nothing, and it tells George which of grandad's notes are within reach for relistening. That turns the voice notes into deliberately chosen comprehensible input. A second number, "you can say 412 of the 1,000 most common Hebrew words", gives an honest progress measure that the app lacks.

Three caveats apply:

- **Show a figure, not a pass/fail.** Comprehension rises in a straight line, so a continuous figure is the right display.
- **Use the app's own word matcher.** The count must run through the same prefix-peeling matcher the practice gate uses, otherwise the number and the gate will disagree.
- **Expect some inaccuracy.** Migaku, the commercial app closest to this idea, withdrew its known-word percentage from generated stories until its accuracy improved ([Migaku changelog](https://migaku.com/blog/changelog)).

**4. Introduce common new words inside easy sentences.** The rule that lets one unready word ride along is currently used for rescuing weak words and for map words, not for introducing new ones. Pointing it at the next high-frequency new word means that word's first sentence arrives with every other word well known and with audio. That is the combination the incidental-learning research favours ([Uchihara et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343)).

The practice gate already allows this; the only requirement is that the word is on his study list first. Keep the word card as well. Deliberate study is the part with the strongest evidence, and the "exactly one unknown" rule has never been tested against alternatives.

**5. Add short connected listening passages per node.** Each map node could open with a short linked story or dialogue on the node's situation, where he knows about 95% of the words, heard before he drills the node. Several sentences on one topic recycle the same words, which lowers the load and adds the repeat encounters that incidental learning needs ([Krashen on narrow reading](https://sdkrashen.com/content/articles/2004_case_for_narrow_reading_lang_mag.pdf); [Gardner 2008](http://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/April2008/gardner/gardner.html)). This is the change that most directly adds the thinnest strand. The "listen" item type already exists.

The caveats:

- **The audio is the phone's own voice.** It has flat intonation, and speech-recognition checks do little for intonation ([ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)).
- **Grandad's notes remain the best native audio he has.**

**6. Let frequency weight the content, and admit chunks.** The last content batch gave "everyday words" four to six sentences each and rare nouns one or two, based on the writer's judgement ([2026-09-23 spec](../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-23-one-list-and-a-bank-that-covers-it-design.md)). The ranking makes that weighting objective, and the sentence writer can be briefed the same way.

Multi-word expressions are a known gap. English research finds that the 505 most common expressions whose meaning isn't the sum of their words would make up over 10% of a 5,000-item list ([Martinez & Schmitt 2012](https://www.lextutor.ca/tests/pvst/martinez_schmitt_2012.pdf)). Meanwhile, the app cannot credit a sentence for using a two-word entry such as *bazman ha'akharon* ("lately") ([hebrew-reader.html](../hebrew-reader.html)). No Hebrew list of such expressions exists, so this one would be built by hand from what grandad says.

**What not to change:**

- **Don't loosen the practice gate.** It is correct for speaking practice.
- **Don't make cards for the glued-on letters or grammar words.** The app already exempts them, which matches the reasoning that these are better learned from input.
- **Don't bulk-import a 5,000-word list into his study list.** That is years of queue.
- **Don't treat any percentage as a gate.**
- **Don't adopt a silent period.** Nothing in the evidence says George should stop speaking.

## Conclusion

The app reached the research's position from the opposite direction. Complaints about hard sentences produced the "every word known but one" rule, and trust in grandad's voice notes produced a personal frequency count. What it never had is a view of Hebrew at large. That is exactly what matters most now: George is at roughly 500 words, new-word slots are scarce, and Duolingo's order has stopped tracking frequency.

A general ranking is scaffolding with a planned end. It should decide the next word until he can say about 2,000–3,000 common dictionary words. After that, grandad's usage should take over as the organising list, the way the mid-frequency research recommends. The other half of the video's advice, more understandable input, points to the app's real gap. The problem is not how hard the sentences are, but how little connected listening sits around them.

The honest limit on all of this: every coverage number and threshold here comes from English. Hebrew has no published coverage study, no tested comprehension threshold and no validated learner word list. Treat the targets as bearings, and trust George's own measured progress over any borrowed percentage.
