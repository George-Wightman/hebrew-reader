# SLA evidence on learning vocabulary by frequency lists

Scope note: almost all of this evidence is about English as an L2, counted against English corpora (BNC, COCA, CANCODE). Direct Hebrew-as-L2 evidence was not found (see Gaps under the counting-unit question). Figures below were checked against the primary papers where marked "(primary text read)"; the rest come from published abstracts.

## 1. Text coverage: how much running text the top N word families cover

### Takeaway
The first 1,000 word families do most of the work: about 78–84% of running words, plus 1–6% from proper nouns. After that each extra 1,000 adds less and less: roughly 8–9% for the second 1,000 in writing (5–6% in speech), 3–5% for the third, and about 3% for the fourth and fifth together. Speech leans on high-frequency words more than writing does. About 3,000 families plus proper nouns gives about 95% of conversation, while writing needs about 4,000 families for 95%. For 98%, speech needs about 6,000–7,000 families and writing about 8,000–9,000.

### Cited Findings
- **Nation (2006), Canadian Modern Language Review 63(1), average coverage by band (Table 14)**. Primary text read.
  - 1st 1,000: 78–81% of written text, 81–84% of spoken.
  - 2nd 1,000: 8–9% written, 5–6% spoken.
  - 3rd 1,000: 3–5% written, 2–3% spoken.
  - 4th–5th 1,000: about 3% written, 1.5–3% spoken.
  - 6th–9th 1,000: about 2% written, 0.75–1% spoken.
  - 10th–14th 1,000: under 1% written, about 0.5% spoken.
  - Proper nouns: 2–4% written, 1–1.5% spoken.
  - Source: [Nation 2006 PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), novels.** Combining five novels: 2,000 families = 87.83%; 4,000 + proper nouns = 94.8%; 9,000 + proper nouns = 98.24%. Nation's summary: "A vocabulary of 8,000 to 9,000 words is needed to read a novel, and even then, 1 word in 50 will be unfamiliar." Most of those unfamiliar words occur only once or twice. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), Lady Chatterley's Lover, cumulative coverage including proper nouns** (the curve flattening, step by step):
  - 1k = 82.93%
  - 2k = 90.14%
  - 3k = 93.28%
  - 4k = 95.06%
  - 5k = 96.13%
  - 6k = 96.88%
  - 7k = 97.43%
  - 8k = 97.90%
  - 9k = 98.22%
  - 10k = 98.46%
  - 14k = 99.01%
  - Nation notes that "from about the sixth 1,000 onwards, each additional 1,000 word-family provides only a small increase in coverage." — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), newspapers.** 2,000 families = about 81.5–84.3% across five corpora. 4,000 + proper nouns = about 94–95.4%. 8,000 + proper nouns = 97.3–98.3%. Proper nouns alone are 4.55–6.12% of newspaper tokens. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), unscripted speech** (Wellington Spoken Corpus: talk-back radio and interviews, then family/friend conversation). Primary text read.
  - 2,000 families = 89.41% and 89.35%.
  - 3,000 + proper nouns = 96.52% and 96.03%.
  - 6,000 + proper nouns = 98.26% and 97.67%.
  - 7,000 + proper nouns = 98.62% and 97.95%.
  - Nation's conclusion: "3,000 word-families plus proper nouns give over 95% coverage, and 6,000 to 7,000 word-families are needed to get 98% coverage."
  - He also cautions that coverage above 98% "may be needed to cope effectively with the transitory nature of spoken language."
  - Source: [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), the film Shrek.** 1k = 83.01%; 3k = 94.28%; 4k = 96.74%; about 7k = 98.08% (all including proper nouns). The script has about 10,000 tokens from about 1,100 families. Beyond the 6th 1,000 it contains 134 families. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), a graded reader.** The Picture of Dorian Gray (Oxford Bookworms Level 3, 1,000-word limit) reaches 96.75% at 2,000 + proper nouns and 98.86% at 3,000 + proper nouns. Graded readers bring authentic-style reading within reach at low vocabulary sizes. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), the conclusion usually quoted.** "If we take 98% as the ideal coverage, a 8,000–9,000 word-family vocabulary is needed for dealing with written text, and 6,000–7,000 families for dealing with spoken text." — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf); [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ750537)
- **Adolphs & Schmitt (2003), Applied Linguistics 24(4).** In the CANCODE spoken corpus, "2,000 word families made up less than 95% coverage." About 5,000 individual words were needed for about 96% coverage of CANCODE plus BNC-spoken. The authors conclude that everyday spoken discourse needs more vocabulary than earlier estimates suggested. Earlier work (Schonell et al.) had claimed about 2,000 words gave about 99% of speech. — [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ678005); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/24/4/425/213596)
- **Webb & Rodgers (2009), Language Learning 59, television.** Corpus of 88 programmes, 264,384 running words.
  - 3,000 families + proper nouns + marginal words = 95.45% coverage.
  - 7,000 families (same additions) = 98.27%.
  - By genre: 95% needs 2,000–4,000 families; 98% needs 5,000–9,000.
  - Source: [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2009.00509.x); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ839281)
- **Ha (2022), a revisit using the BNC/COCA lists over about 626 million words of subtitles:**

  | Genre | Families for 95% | Families for 98% |
  |---|---|---|
  | Soap operas | 2,000 | 4,000 |
  | Movies | 2,000 | 5,000 |
  | TV programmes | 3,000 | 5,000 |

  - Proper nouns, marginal words, transparent compounds and acronyms together make up about 11% of soap-opera tokens and about 6% of movie and TV tokens.
  - The first two 1,000-family bands covered nearly 90% of tokens.
  - Source: [Ha 2022, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8899723)

### Inferences
- Top-100 figure: I did not find a primary source giving top-100 coverage in the papers retrieved (see Gaps). The first 1,000 alone carries about 80%, so function words dominate the very top of any list.
- For a learner at "a few hundred words", the gains from the next 1,000–2,000 high-frequency items are large: going from about 1,000 to 2,000 adds roughly 5–9 percentage points of coverage. No later band comes close to that return.
- Spoken and TV language is the most "frequency-friendly" register. A spoken-first learner reaches workable coverage sooner than a reader of newspapers, which carry heavy proper-noun and topic loads.

### Gaps
- **Top-100 coverage.** I found no primary-source figure for the top 100 families in these papers. The often-quoted "top 100 words cover about 50% of text" claim was not verified in this search and should not be stated as a finding.
- **Newer counts.** Coverage figures under the newer BNC/COCA 25k lists for general written text were not retrieved. Only Nation 2006 (BNC lists) and Ha 2022 (subtitles) were.

## 2. Comprehension thresholds: 95% vs 98%, and what 80–90% coverage looks like

### Takeaway
There is no sharp threshold. The strongest evidence (Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011, n = 661; Kremmel et al. 2023, a registered replication) shows comprehension rising roughly linearly with coverage. "95%" and "98%" are points where a *particular* definition of "adequate" comprehension becomes likely; they are not cliffs. At 90% coverage, learners still get roughly half of a text. At 80%, comprehension is poor but not zero. The 98% figure for *reading* failed to replicate in 2023. For *listening*, 90–95% appears workable.

### Cited Findings
- **Laufer (1989)**, as summarised in Schmitt et al. 2011 (primary text read).
  - 95% coverage was the point that best separated learners who scored 55% on a reading comprehension test from those who did not.
  - 55% was the lowest pass mark at the University of Haifa.
  - Laufer tied this to about 5,000 words, using Dutch frequency counts (Ostyn & Godin).
  - Schmitt et al. criticise the 55% criterion as "very modest" and the Dutch-to-English transfer as unclear.
  - The 95% figure was "defined probabilistically in terms of group success" and was not meant as a threshold.
  - Source: [Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011 PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)
- **Hu & Nation (2000)**, fiction text, n = 66, pre-university students in New Zealand.
  - Mean scores by coverage:

    | Coverage | Multiple choice (out of 14) | Written recall (out of 124) |
    |---|---|---|
    | 80% | 6.06 | 24.60 |
    | 90% | 9.50 | 51.31 |
    | 95% | 10.18 | 61.00 |
    | 100% | 12.24 | 77.17 |

  - Adequate comprehension (12/14 multiple choice; 70/124 written recall): no learner reached it at 80%, 3 of 16 (19%) at 90%, and 35–41% of learners at 95%.
  - A regression gave 98% as the level where most learners would comprehend adequately. Nobody was actually tested at 98%.
  - Source: [Schmitt et al. 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf); [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Kurnia (2003)**, a non-fiction text: few L2 learners reached adequate comprehension even at 98% coverage (cited in Nation 2006). Nation adds that "even 98% coverage does not make comprehension easy." — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011)**, Modern Language Journal 95(1), 661 participants from 8 countries, two academic texts. Primary text read.
  - The relationship is "essentially linear, at least within the vocabulary coverage range of 90% to 100%", with "no obvious point at which comprehension dramatically accelerated."
  - Comprehension went from about 50% at 90% coverage (mean 15.15/30 = 50.5%) to about 75% at 100% coverage (22.58/30 = 75.3%).
  - Their mapping: 60% comprehension needs 95% coverage; 70% needs 98–99%; 75% needs 100%.
  - They conclude 98% is "a more reasonable coverage target for readers of academic texts."
  - Knowing every word still gave only about 75%, because vocabulary is necessary but not sufficient.
  - The correlation between coverage and comprehension was only about .41.
  - Source: [PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2011.01146.x)
- **Low-coverage comprehension, from the same paper.** In Hu & Nation, learners at 80% coverage still scored about 43% on multiple choice and about 20% on written recall; at 90%, 68% and 41%. Schmitt et al.: "although comprehension may not be easy when there is more than 1 unknown word in 10, learners can still achieve substantial comprehension." — [Schmitt et al. 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)
- **Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010)**, Reading in a Foreign Language 22(1), University of Haifa. They propose two thresholds:
  - "Optimal": 8,000 families, giving 98% coverage (including proper nouns).
  - "Minimal": 4,000–5,000 families, giving 95%.
  - Source: [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887873.pdf); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ887873)
- **Kremmel, Indrarathne, Kormos & Suzuki (2023)**, Language Learning 73(4), a pre-registered replication of Hu & Nation with 104 Sri Lankan adult learners. Primary text read.
  - It "confirmed the original finding of a mostly linear relationship between vocabulary coverage and reading comprehension but failed to replicate an inferred 98% coverage threshold as sufficient for adequate comprehension."
  - Predicted accuracy on the narrative multiple-choice task: 80% coverage = 0.50; 90% = 0.71; 95% = 0.68; 98% = 0.73; 100% = 0.80.
  - Genre and question format mattered a great deal. Open-ended questions and informational texts were harder at the same lexical profile.
  - Their stated 85% "adequate" level was not reached in any condition.
  - They question "whether a one-size-fits-all threshold at any vocabulary coverage percentage value is warranted."
  - They also note that word-family profiling "might overestimate students' vocabulary size."
  - Source: [Open-access PDF](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12622)
- **Kremmel et al. (2023) on the replication record.** Larson's 2016 approximate replication of Hu & Nation also "do[es] not seem to confirm" the original. — [Kremmel et al. 2023](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724)
- **van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013)**, Applied Linguistics 34(4), listening.
  - 90–95% lexical coverage may support adequate listening comprehension: mean scores were about 73.5% at 90% coverage and 76% at 95%.
  - They lean toward 95% because scores varied less there than at 90%.
  - At 100% coverage, 29 of 40 participants scored perfectly.
  - Source: [ERIC review PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1316858.pdf); [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/34/4/457/199564); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/270815130_Lexical_Coverage_in_L1_and_L2_Listening_Comprehension_The_Same_or_Different_from_Reading_Comprehension)
- **Webb & Rodgers (2009)** also suggested that 95% may do for TV viewing, and lower may be enough given the visual support (per the Kremmel et al. 2023 literature review). — [Kremmel et al. 2023](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724)

### Inferences
- **Status of the evidence.**
  - Strong: comprehension rises steadily with coverage.
  - Contested or weak: any exact threshold. The famous 98% came from a regression on 66 people and did not replicate cleanly.
- **For a learner at a few hundred words.** Authentic Hebrew speech or text will sit well below 80% coverage, the range where comprehension collapses. Graded or adapted material, and speech with strong visual or situational support, is where input becomes usable.
- **In practice.** The "2% unknown" ideal is a good target for *extensive* (fluent, pleasure) reading. The "5–10% unknown" range is workable for *intensive* study with support.

### Gaps
- **Hebrew thresholds.** No study was found measuring coverage-comprehension thresholds for Hebrew as L2. Unpointed script (no vowels) plausibly raises the processing cost of each unknown word, but I found no evidence either way.

## 3. Diminishing returns and the "mid-frequency" argument

### Takeaway
The frequency curve flattens fast. Past about 3,000 families, each extra 1,000 buys about 1–3% coverage in writing and less in speech, and the words are spread thinly across texts. Schmitt & Schmitt (2014) redraw the bands as high-frequency 0–3,000, mid-frequency 3,000–9,000, and low-frequency 9,000+. They argue the mid band is necessary for real proficiency but too large and too thinly spread to teach item by item. Topic words also cut across frequency bands: a text's recurring topic words are often "low-frequency" on a general list.

### Cited Findings
- **Schmitt & Schmitt (2014)**, Language Teaching 47, 484–503. Primary text not read.
  - High-frequency vocabulary should be the top 3,000 word families, not the traditional 2,000.
  - Low-frequency should start at 9,000 rather than 10,000.
  - 3,000–9,000 is "mid-frequency vocabulary", needed for proficient use but posing a distinct teaching challenge.
  - Sources: [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-reassessment-of-frequency-and-vocabulary-size-in-Schmitt-Schmitt/b032abfe15a34dbd444d3b8ae5cc478033c0958e); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1040866); [Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/734831/a-reassessment-of-frequency-and-vocabulary-size-in-l2-vocabulary-teaching)
- **Nation (2006), how few words each band contributes per text.** In Lady Chatterley's Lover, the 6th 1,000 contributed 832 tokens from 364 types across 263 families, with "most of the types at this level occurred only once in the novel." In Shrek, the 6th 1,000 contributed 33 tokens from 27 types. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), the variation is in the top band.** "The greatest variation in vocabulary coverage is most likely to occur in the first 1,000 words, and in the proper nouns." Within a genre, first-1,000 coverage varies by no more than 2–3%. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Nation (2006), topic words.** Low-frequency topic words recur within a single text: ogre, swamp, donkey, dragon and knight in Shrek. Once learned early in the text, they behave like known words. Nation tested whether treating them as known reduces the vocabulary size needed. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Webb & Rodgers (2009)** analysed the number of *encounters* with low-frequency words in TV. Their argument: once learners know about 3,000 families, watching TV regularly can drive incidental learning of further vocabulary. — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2009.00509.x)

### Inferences
- **Where a general list stops paying off.** Deliberate list study is most efficient up to about 2,000–3,000 families (or the lemma equivalent). Beyond that, ranking by *general* frequency has less value than choosing words by the learner's own domains and texts, and than volume of input.
- **A mid-frequency word is rarely met again in general input.** Card study or targeted topic input has to make up for that. So the argument is not to stop deliberate study after 3k. It is to stop letting a *general* rank decide what to study.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the full Schmitt & Schmitt paper, so I could not quote its specific recommendations for teaching mid-frequency words (e.g. extensive reading volume, graded readers at higher levels, "mid-frequency readers"). The Hsu (2019) paper on VOA news as reading material for mid-frequency vocabulary was seen only as a title: [Hsu 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0033688218764460).

## 4. Counting-unit problems: word family vs lemma vs flemma vs word form, and why morphology matters

### Takeaway
Every coverage figure above counts *word families* (Bauer & Nation Level 6), which fold all inflections and many derivations into one item. That assumes the learner can recognise nation, nationalistic and internationalisation as "one word". There is growing evidence and argument that learners, especially lower-level ones, cannot, so family counts overstate what a given list size delivers. Recent lists use lemmas (Brezina & Gablasova's New-GSL) or flemmas (Dang & Webb's Essential Word List). For a morphologically rich, templatic language like Hebrew the choice of unit changes the numbers dramatically. Separately, frequency does not equal learnability.

### Cited Findings
- **Nation (2006) on the family unit.** A Level-6 family can be large: nation includes national, nationalism, internationalisation, nationalistically and 20+ other members. Nation justifies Level 6 as groupings "that would be perceived as the same words for the receptive skills" and says "Level 6 is an acceptable level for advanced learners." He concedes that for productive purposes "the lemma would be the largest sensible unit to use, because each lemma takes different collocates and different grammatical patterns." — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Brezina & Gablasova (2015)**, Applied Linguistics 36(1), the New General Service List.
  - 2,494 *lemmas* drawn from four corpora totalling over 12 billion words.
  - Coverage is about 80%, comparable to West's GSL, with far fewer forms.
  - They chose lemmas because of "semantic distance" between members of a family, and doubts that learners have the morphological skills to handle derivational relationships.
  - Sources: [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/36/1/1/226623); [EAP Foundation summary](https://www.eapfoundation.com/vocab/general/newgsl/)
- **Dang & Webb (2016)** used the flemma for their Essential Word List. A flemma groups a form's inflections across parts of speech, so *reading* as verb and noun is one item. A review of counting units argues that smaller units (types, lemmas) suit early learners and word families suit advanced ones. — [VocabLevelTest help, counting units](https://sites.google.com/view/vocableveltestorghelp/test-features/word-lists/word-counting-units); [Brown, "Examining the word family through word lists"](https://www.castledown.com/journals/vli/article/view/vli.v07.1.brown/426)
- **"The lemma dilemma" and related papers** (Studies in Second Language Acquisition, c. 2021). These debate whether a correct answer on one family member can be taken as knowledge of the whole family, and a study with Japanese EFL learners found the flemma appropriate for most of them. I could not access the primary texts: the Cambridge page returned 403, so authors and exact findings are unverified here. — [SSLA "The lemma dilemma"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/lemma-dilemma/7A0EA6CD155775FB34F55908DC62292A); [ResearchGate: "Is the lemma more appropriate than the flemma?"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345521275_Is_the_Lemma_More_Appropriate_than_the_Flemma_as_a_Word_Counting_Unit)
- **Kremmel et al. (2023)** list "the inadequacy of word family based lexical profiling that might overestimate students' vocabulary size" as a possible reason learners failed to comprehend texts supposedly within their range. — [Kremmel et al. 2023](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724)
- **Kremmel et al. (2023) on frequency vs difficulty.** Text difficulty "is not solely predicted by lexical complexity"; topic familiarity, background knowledge, syntax and cohesion all matter. At the word level, orthography–phonology complexity, imageability and concreteness affect difficulty too (citing Crossley et al. 2022). — [Kremmel et al. 2023](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724)
- **Hebrew-specific (limited).** A Hebrew study on core vocabulary for augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) reports that a 10-million-word Hebrew corpus contained 2,300 different morphological patterns against 25,000 different lemmas. It argues that Hebrew's rich Semitic morphology must be considered when selecting core words. — [Frequency of word usage by Hebrew preschoolers, T&F 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07434618.2023.2210671). Note: this is an AAC and L1-child study, not L2.
- **Hebrew-specific: root processing.** Spoken-word recognition in Hebrew draws on the root: earlier access to root information enabled recognition at earlier gates in an auditory gating study. — [PMC 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9221200/)

### Inferences
These are my reasoning. None were tested in the sources.
- **Orthographic word forms badly inflate Hebrew counts.** In Hebrew, the article ה, the conjunction ו, the prepositions ב/ל/כ/מ, the relative ש and pronominal suffixes all attach to the written word. So one lemma (בית) appears as many written forms: בבית, הבית, לבית, והבית, ביתו, and so on. A frequency list built on *orthographic word forms* would both inflate list sizes and fragment frequency. Any Hebrew list used for study should be lemmatised.
- **The root is a word-family analogue, but a much looser one than English derivation.** Words sharing a root across binyanim/mishkalim can drift far apart in meaning (e.g. roots yielding both concrete and abstract items). Counting "root families" would overstate a learner's knowledge far more than English Level-6 families do. The lemma, or at most the flemma, is the defensible unit for an early learner, and English family-based figures should not be carried over to Hebrew numerically.
- **Root awareness may still help learning.** If root recognition helps processing, as the gating study suggests for native speakers, then *once* a learner has a critical mass of lemmas, root awareness could speed learning of new words. That makes the "family" idea useful as a learning strategy rather than as a counting unit.

### Gaps
- **Hebrew coverage figures.** I found no published Hebrew-L2 coverage study of the form "top N lemmas cover X% of Hebrew conversation, subtitles or news", and no Hebrew-L2 comprehension-threshold study.
- **Hebrew frequency lists.** I found no Hebrew frequency list designed for L2 learners with documented methodology. A Routledge "Frequency Dictionary" for Modern Hebrew was not found in this search.
- **Arabic-speaking learners.** A corpus study of Arabic-speaking learners' productive Hebrew vocabulary in higher-education essays exists (mentioned in search results), but I could not retrieve its details.

## 5. Critiques and limits of frequency lists

### Takeaway
Frequency lists have four well-supported limits. First, they depend on the corpus: coverage varies by genre, and proper nouns alone range from about 1% of speech to about 6% of news. Second, their counting unit is debatable (section 4). Third, they ignore multiword units that carry meanings their parts do not. Fourth, a word's rank says nothing about how hard it is to learn. That list learning is inferior to learning in context is *not* well supported as a blanket claim (section 6). The better-supported view is that the two do different jobs.

### Cited Findings
- **Corpus dependence (Nation 2006).**
  - The first 2,000 families cover about 88% of novels but about 83% of newspapers.
  - Proper nouns are 4.55–6.12% of newspaper tokens against about 1% of conversation.
  - Topic-specific corpora distort ranks: "Zealand" and "Wellington" sit in the 9th 1,000 and "Maori" in the 14th. The Wellington corpus would show about 0.4% more high-frequency coverage if those were ranked higher.
  - Fillers and interjections (um, er, oh, aah) sit in the 3rd 1,000 of the BNC lists, which inflates spoken 3k coverage.
  - Source: [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **List-making methods also differ.** Adolphs & Schmitt counted the words that actually occurred most in their spoken corpus, while Nation applied external BNC lists. So "top 2,000" means different items in different studies. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **Multiword units: Martinez & Schmitt (2012), the PHRASE List**, Applied Linguistics 33(3), 299–320.
  - 505 of the most frequent *non-transparent* multiword expressions in English, intended for receptive use.
  - "If integrated into and calculated as part of the 5,000 most frequent word families, the 505 multiword items would constitute over 10 per cent of the total items."
  - Single-word lists therefore miss a meaningful slice of high-frequency meaning units, the ones whose meaning cannot be built from known words.
  - Sources: [PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/tests/pvst/martinez_schmitt_2012.pdf); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ972496)
- **Frequency is not learnability.** Genre and question type changed comprehension at identical coverage. Word-level features beyond frequency (imageability, concreteness, spelling–sound complexity) also affect difficulty. — [Kremmel et al. 2023](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724)
- **Coverage is not comprehension.** Even at 100% coverage, advanced learners averaged about 75% comprehension, and the coverage–comprehension correlation was only about .41. — [Schmitt et al. 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Function words.** The very top of any list is function words and grammatical morphemes. In Hebrew many of these are clitics, not separate words (ו, ה, ב, ל, ש), so a flashcard of "ב = in" teaches very little. These items are plausibly better acquired through large amounts of input and grammar focus than through isolated cards. This is an inference: I found no study directly comparing card vs input learning of function words.
- **For Hebrew, prefer subtitle or spoken corpora.** Newspaper-based Hebrew lists will over-weight political and proper-noun vocabulary relative to conversation. Subtitle-based lists (e.g. OpenSubtitles-derived) are the closest proxy for everyday speech. This is supported by the English genre differences above, not by Hebrew data.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved that directly tests "function words learned via input vs flashcards." I found nothing retrievable on it.

## 6. What deliberate learning (flashcards, spaced repetition) adds over incidental acquisition

### Takeaway
Deliberate learning is efficient and durable. Flashcards and word lists are among the most effective word-focused activities, at about 60% average gains on immediate tests. There is also evidence that deliberately learned words become real, automatically processed lexical entries, not just declarative facts. Incidental learning from input works but is slow and needs repeated encounters: repetition correlates with learning only moderately (r = .34). The mainstream position (Nation's Four Strands) is that deliberate study should be *one* strand, about 25%, alongside meaning-focused input, output and fluency work.

### Cited Findings
- **Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020)**, Modern Language Journal 104(4), 715–738, a meta-analysis.
  - 100 effect sizes from 22 studies, covering flashcards, word lists, writing and fill-in-the-blanks.
  - Mean gains were 60.1% on meaning recall and 58.5% on form recall on immediate posttests.
  - Across activities, gains ranged from 18.4% to 77.0% immediately and 23.9% to 73.4% on delayed tests.
  - Flashcards and word lists gave "relatively large gains"; writing and fill-in-the-blanks gave "relatively small gains."
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12671); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346001854_How_Effective_Are_Intentional_Vocabulary-Learning_Activities_A_Meta-Analysis)
- **Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019)**, Language Learning 69, 559–599, a meta-analysis of 45 effect sizes from 26 studies (N = 1,918).
  - There is a medium effect (r = .34) of number of encounters on incidental vocabulary learning.
  - The effect is moderated by learner variables (age, vocabulary knowledge) and treatment variables.
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1224114)
- **Uchihara and colleagues also have a companion meta-analysis** on how effective incidental learning is overall, in Language Teaching (c. 2023). I saw it only as a title and did not read the effect sizes. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-effective-is-second-language-incidental-vocabulary-learning-a-metaanalysis/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C)
- **Elgort (2011)**, Language Learning.
  - 48 pseudowords were learned deliberately with flashcards: about 4 hours spread over one week, by intermediate and advanced bilinguals.
  - Afterwards they showed form priming, masked repetition priming and semantic priming effects.
  - They were processed more automatically than nonwords and low-frequency real L2 words.
  - Conclusion: deliberate learning "triggered the acquisition of representational and functional aspects of vocabulary knowledge."
  - Proposed replications were published in Language Teaching in 2022.
  - Sources: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00613.x); [PDF](https://www.lextutor.ca/freq/lists_download/elgort_2011.pdf); [replication proposal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/word-knowledge-learning-and-acquisition-in-a-second-language-proposed-replications-of-elgort-2011-and-qiao-and-forster-2017/BC4A26C7F6CF77137FC114E1BE8C39D9)
- **Nation's Four Strands** (Nation 2007, Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching 1(1)).
  - A balanced course gives roughly equal time to four strands: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning (which includes deliberate vocabulary study) and fluency development.
  - That puts deliberate study at about 25% of time.
  - Sources: [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2167/illt039.0); [Yamamoto, "Applying the four strands", VUW](https://www.victoria.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0003/1626123/2012-Yamamoto-Four-strands.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Deliberate study is a legitimate main route for high-frequency lemmas.** The evidence answers the worry that flashcard words don't become "real" vocabulary: Elgort shows they do at the level of automatic lexical processing, though only in a small lab study. It also shows deliberate learning is efficient.
- **Incidental learning needs coverage first.** It depends on many encounters (r = .34 with repetition) and on already knowing the surrounding text, which ties back to section 2. That makes it the natural engine *after* the high-frequency base is in place, and the main route for mid-frequency words met in the learner's own input.
- **The combination is what's recommended, not either alone.** Deliberately learned words need to be met in meaning-focused input and used in fluency practice to consolidate: form, collocation, register and grammar patterns are not taught by a card.

### Gaps
- **Spaced-repetition specifics.** SRS parameters (spacing schedules, retrieval practice) were not specifically researched here, and I retrieved no SRS-specific L2 meta-analysis.
- **Delayed retention of flashcard learning.** The Webb et al. 2020 figures give the range of delayed gains across activities but not a flashcard-specific delayed mean.

## 7. Concrete recommendations researchers give

### Takeaway
The consistent research-derived recommendation has three parts:
1. Deliberately learn the high-frequency words first: traditionally 2,000 families, now more often 3,000 (Schmitt & Schmitt 2014). For early learners, count them as lemmas or flemmas, not families.
2. Move early to graded or adapted input, which lets learners read at 95–98% coverage with a small vocabulary.
3. Use large volumes of meaning-focused input (reading, TV, listening) to meet mid-frequency words, supported by targeted deliberate study, within a balanced Four-Strands diet.

### Cited Findings
- **Graded readers work with a small vocabulary.** A Level-3 Oxford Bookworm (1,000-word limit) reaches 96.75% coverage with 2,000 families + proper nouns and 98.86% with 3,000 + proper nouns. Graded readers let learners read at the target coverage long before they reach the 8,000–9,000 needed for unsimplified novels. — [Nation 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)
- **The high-frequency target is 3,000 families** (revised up from 2,000); mid-frequency (3,000–9,000) needs its own strategy. — [Schmitt & Schmitt 2014, ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1040866)
- **Viewing becomes viable at about 3,000 families.** With 3,000 families + proper nouns and marginal words, TV reaches about 95% coverage, and regular viewing becomes a viable source of incidental learning. — [Webb & Rodgers 2009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2009.00509.x); [Ha 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8899723)
- **Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski's (2010) two targets:** 4,000–5,000 families as the minimal reading threshold (95%), and 8,000 as optimal (98%). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887873.pdf)
- **Set time with Nation's Four Strands:** about a quarter of learning time on deliberate language-focused work, including vocabulary cards, and the rest on meaning-focused input, output and fluency. — [Nation 2007, T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2167/illt039.0)
- **Add high-frequency non-transparent multiword expressions** to single-word study, via the PHRASE List. — [Martinez & Schmitt 2012](https://www.lextutor.ca/tests/pvst/martinez_schmitt_2012.pdf)
- **Treat thresholds as guides, not gates,** because comprehension rises continuously and depends on text and task. — [Kremmel et al. 2023](https://ulb-dok.uibk.ac.at/ulbtirolfodok/download/pdf/9760724); [Schmitt et al. 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)

### Inferences (applied to an adult English speaker at a few hundred words of Modern Hebrew)
- **"Learn the frequency list" is well supported as the main deliberate-study policy right now.** From a few hundred to about 2,000–3,000 lemmas is the highest-return stretch in the whole curve: each 1,000 in this range adds more coverage than every band beyond 5,000 put together.
- **It should use a lemmatised, spoken or subtitle-based Hebrew list.** Orthographic forms mis-count clitic-attached words. Root "families" overstate knowledge.
- **It should run alongside heavy comprehensible input from the start.** That means graded or adapted material, and speech with visual support. Clitic function words and grammatical morphology are better learned there.
- **It stops being the organising principle around 3,000 lemmas.** From there, word choice should follow the learner's own input and domains, the topic words that recur in what he actually reads and hears. Card study becomes a way to lock in words met in context rather than a march down a general rank.
- **Multiword chunks deserve cards too.** Hebrew has its own high-frequency non-transparent expressions. I found no Hebrew PHRASE-style list; that is a gap.

### Gaps
- **Nation's own wording.** I did not retrieve a primary-source quotation of Nation's specific "first 2,000 then graded readers" formulation. The graded-reader coverage data above supports it, but the quote itself remains unverified.
- **Hebrew teaching materials.** I found no Hebrew-specific graded-reader coverage data, and no research-based Hebrew L2 vocabulary syllabus.
