# Comprehensible input, frequency-based vocabulary, and how apps combine them

Scope: (a) the CI hypothesis, its evidence and critiques, and where the field stands in 2025-2026; (b) the "frequency list, then input" method from online language-learning circles; (c) product patterns that estimate known words and how comprehensible a text is; (d) where output and speaking fit, for a learner who mostly speaks sentences aloud and is checked by speech recognition.

Research date: 2026-09-28. The YouTube and community sources (Refold, Dreaming Spanish, Migaku, LingQ, the MorphMan docs) are recorded as **claims**. Peer-reviewed work is recorded as **evidence**. Each entry says which it is.

---

## 1. What the input hypothesis claims, what the evidence says, and where the field stands in 2025-2026

### Takeaway
Nobody seriously disputes that you need a lot of comprehensible input. Krashen's stronger claims are what the field rejects: that input is *sufficient*, and that consciously learned knowledge cannot turn into acquired knowledge. The mainstream position in 2026 is "input plus": lots of meaning-focused input, alongside output and interaction, deliberate study of words and forms, and fluency practice. Meta-analyses of explicit instruction and of deliberate vocabulary study both show real, sizeable effects, and on a strict reading of Krashen those effects should not exist.

### Cited Findings
- **The core claims.** Acquisition happens through input slightly above the learner's current level ("i+1"). Learners use context to fill the gap. Refold's popular version puts this at understanding about 70-90% of what you hear. **This is a claim, not a research threshold.** — [Refold Roadmap Library: Comprehensible Input](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/comprehensible-input)
- **Refold cites Long too.** Its own library leans on Long's Interaction Hypothesis (1996) as well as Krashen, saying input "combined with the opportunity to negotiate meaning" works best. So even the best-known online CI roadmap does not claim input alone is enough. — [Refold Roadmap Library: Comprehensible Input](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/comprehensible-input)
- **Swain's Output Hypothesis.** This came out of Canadian French-immersion data: learners who had years of comprehensible input still lacked grammatical accuracy. Swain argued that learners need "pushed output" to:
  - notice gaps in what they can say,
  - test their hypotheses about the language,
  - develop accuracy.

  She also says output has a metalinguistic function that helps learners "control and internalize linguistic knowledge". — [JALT: Output and Beyond to Dialogue, review of Swain](https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2198-output-and-beyond-dialogue-review-merrill-swains-current-approach-sla); summary also in [Grokipedia: Comprehensible output](https://grokipedia.com/page/Comprehensible_output) (a secondary source)
- **Long's Interaction Hypothesis.** Acquisition is driven by the adjustments people make while negotiating meaning: clarification requests, confirmation checks and comprehension checks. — [Cambridge Language Teaching: Interaction and instructed SLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/interaction-and-instructed-second-language-acquisition/78A156EE200F744F5978F99BFB073DBE)
- **Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis.** Conscious perception of linguistic form (noticing) is necessary for L2 learning, so input the learner does not attend to does not become intake. This goes directly against Krashen's view that acquisition is subconscious. — [Columbia SALT: Input Processing in SLA](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/download/1497/548/3762)
- **Explicit versus implicit instruction.** Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella (2015) re-ran and updated Norris & Ortega's (2000) meta-analysis with 34 unique samples (11 original studies plus 23 new ones). Explicit instruction came out ahead of implicit instruction. The oral-plus-written combination worked best for both. — [John Benjamins: Norris & Ortega (2000) revisited and updated](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Implicit-and-explicit-instruction-in-L2-learning-Goo-Granena/aad1a1dfd2eb49451885ed21a4a78075fc5d7c9e). A secondary search summary gave an explicit-instruction effect of g = 1.443 on free production. **I could not check this against the paper; treat the exact figure as unconfirmed.**
- **A recent strong critique.** A 2025 Frontiers in Psychology paper, "Beyond comprehensible input: a neuro-ecological critique", calls CI "conceptually flawed, empirically outdated, and practically insufficient". It argues acquisition is active and interaction-dependent, and that a "one-size-fits-all i+1" ignores individual learners' paths, which adaptive systems could address. **This is a position/critique paper, not new experimental data; give it the weight of an argument, not a finding.** — [Frontiers 2025, PMC copy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12577063/)
- **Recurring textbook criticisms.** i+1 is not operationally defined (there is no way to measure "i"). The theory does not explain the natural order it relies on. It sets aside output, practice and formal instruction. — [Review of Krashen's Input Theory (drpress EHSS)](https://drpress.org/ojs/index.php/EHSS/article/view/17528); [Critical Evaluation of Krashen's Theory (PNR Journal)](https://www.pnrjournal.com/index.php/home/article/download/9094/12463/10912). **These are low-tier journals: fine for listing the standard critiques, not for anything empirical.**
- **Nation's Four Strands.** This is the most widely cited mainstream framework for how to spend study time. A balanced course gives roughly equal time (about 25% each) to four strands:
  - meaning-focused input,
  - meaning-focused output,
  - language-focused learning (deliberate study of vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation),
  - fluency development (only familiar material, used quickly).

  — [Nation, The Four Strands (Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching, 2007)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2167/illt039.0); practitioner summary in [Hacking Chinese](https://www.hackingchinese.com/analyse-and-balance-your-chinese-learning-with-paul-nations-four-strands/)

### Inferences
- The field's consensus is best read as "input is necessary, and not sufficient". For designing an app, Nation's Four Strands is a better organising principle than pure CI. Pure CI, as Dreaming Spanish teaches it, is one strand pushed to nearly 100%.
- The meta-analyses showing explicit instruction and deliberate vocabulary study work (see §3) are the empirical reason to drop Krashen's "no interface" claim. A frequency deck is only defensible if learned knowledge *can* feed acquisition, and the evidence says it can.
- Schmidt's noticing has a direct design consequence: input that highlights the target word or form helps in principle. Examples are colour-coding unknown words and showing a translation on tap.

### Gaps
- I did not get a verified effect-size table from Goo et al. (2015), and did not check the g = 1.443 figure. The paper is paywalled.
- I did not find direct 2025-2026 quotes from Lightbown & Spada (*How Languages are Learned*, 5th ed.) or from VanPatten. VanPatten's processing-instruction position, roughly "input is central, but learners need help processing form-meaning links", comes from background knowledge and is not sourced here.
- I found no controlled study comparing a pure-CI programme (Dreaming Spanish style) with a mixed approach in adults. Almost all Dreaming Spanish outcome data is self-reported by the community.

## 2. What the research says about extensive reading and listening, lexical coverage, and narrow reading

### Takeaway
Extensive reading reliably improves vocabulary and reading, with meta-analytic effects from small to large. The "98% coverage" figure the online community uses as a comprehensibility threshold comes from one small study (Hu & Nation 2000). A pre-registered replication in 2023 found a *linear* relationship with no threshold. Narrow reading (many texts on one topic) does recycle vocabulary, but the benefits are more complex than Krashen suggested.

### Cited Findings
**Coverage thresholds**
- **Hu & Nation (2000).** 98% coverage (about one unknown word in 50) is the level at which most learners can read unassisted. At 95% some learners reach minimally acceptable comprehension. At 80% nobody did. — [ERIC: Lexical text coverage, learners' vocabulary size](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887873.pdf); [ERIC: Coverage comprehension model](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1296462.pdf)
- **Laufer (1989).** At 95% coverage most learners reached "reasonable" comprehension, defined as scoring at least 55% on a comprehension test. Laufer and Hu & Nation used different definitions of "adequate" comprehension, so both figures can be correct. — [ERIC EJ887873](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887873.pdf)
- **Commonly cited targets.** 98% for reading (Hu & Nation 2000; Schmitt et al. 2011). About 95% may be enough for listening. — [ERIC EJ1296462](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1296462.pdf); see also [Cambridge SSLA: Lexical coverage in L1 and L2 viewing comprehension](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/lexical-coverage-in-l1-and-l2-viewing-comprehension/DFCA6605076705D5762C98F286D16B27)
- **Kremmel, Indrarathne, Kormos & Suzuki (2023), *Language Learning*.** A pre-registered replication of Hu & Nation with 104 Sri Lankan adult learners (the original had 66). They found a **linear relationship between the percentage of known vocabulary and comprehension, with no threshold** below which comprehension collapses. They caution that the figure is "not necessarily 98%". — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12622); [York Research Database](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/unknown-vocabulary-density-and-reading-comprehension-replicating-); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375993564_Unknown_Vocabulary_Density_and_Reading_Comprehension_Replicating_Hu_and_Nation_2000)
  - One secondary wiki credits this to "Kremmel & Schmitt (2023)", which is wrong. The authors are Kremmel, Indrarathne, Kormos & Suzuki. — [tkgally/je-dict-1 research wiki](https://github.com/tkgally/je-dict-1/blob/main/planning/wiki/research/vocabulary-size-coverage.md), contradicted by [York Research Database](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/unknown-vocabulary-density-and-reading-comprehension-replicating-)
- **Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011)** also reported a roughly linear coverage-comprehension relationship rather than a sharp threshold. — cited via the [tkgally research wiki](https://github.com/tkgally/je-dict-1/blob/main/planning/wiki/research/vocabulary-size-coverage.md) (secondary source)

**Vocabulary size and coverage (English, Nation 2006, BNC word families)**

| Coverage target | Novels | Newspapers | Spoken English |
|---|---|---|---|
| 95% | about 4,000 families | about 4,000 plus proper nouns | about 3,000 plus proper nouns |
| 98% | about 9,000 families | 8,000-9,000 plus proper nouns | 6,000-7,000 |

Rough coverage by vocabulary size:
- 1,000 families: about 72-78%
- 2,000 families: about 80-85%
- 3,000 families: about 86-90%

— [tkgally research wiki summarising Nation 2006](https://github.com/tkgally/je-dict-1/blob/main/planning/wiki/research/vocabulary-size-coverage.md) (secondary). Refold's roadmap similarly claims "the most common 1000 words make up about 70% of any language". **The "any language" part is a claim.** — [Refold 0C](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-0/c/active-study/)

**Extensive reading and incidental learning**
- **Jeon & Day (2016)** meta-analysed 49 studies: 51 experimental-versus-control effect sizes and 20 pre-post effect sizes. Extensive reading had a positive effect, ranging from small to large depending on duration and outcome. — [ERIC EJ1117026](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1117026.pdf); [NFLRC RFL item](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/354)
- **Liu & Zhang (2018)** meta-analysed 21 studies of extensive reading and vocabulary: d = 1.32 for group contrasts and d = 3.26 for pre-post. — [ERIC EJ1179114](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1179114.pdf); [CCSE ELT](https://www.ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/elt/article/view/75243)
- **A 2025 meta-analysis** in *Educational Psychology Review* ("Learning a Language Through Reading") updates the extensive-reading evidence base. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10068-6). **I did not extract its effect sizes.**
- **Repetition and incidental learning.** Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019) meta-analysed 26 studies (45 effects, N = 1,918) and found a **medium correlation (r = .34)** between the number of encounters and incidental vocabulary learning. It was moderated by:
  - learner age and existing vocabulary knowledge,
  - spacing,
  - visual support,
  - engagement,
  - the range of encounter counts.

  — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343); [ERIC EJ1224114](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1224114)

**Narrow reading and listening**
- **Krashen's case.** Staying within one topic or author gives repeated exposure to related vocabulary and context. He proposed narrow reading (1981/2004) and narrow listening (1996). — [Krashen, The Case for Narrow Reading (2004)](https://sdkrashen.com/content/articles/2004_case_for_narrow_reading_lang_mag.pdf)
- **Schmitt & Carter (2000).** Texts on one topic contain fewer word types because key words and proper nouns recur, which lowers the lexical load. — [Gianfranco Conti summary](https://gianfrancoconti.com/2015/06/21/narrow-reading-and-narrow-listening-enhancing-receptive-skills-through-focused-and-purposeful-recycling/)
- **Gardner (2008)**, a corpus study of children's authentic texts, found narrow reading has real advantages for recycling specialised vocabulary, "but that such advantages may be much more complex than previously indicated". — [Reading in a Foreign Language, April 2008](http://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/April2008/gardner/gardner.html)

### Inferences
- **For an app, "98% known" is a useful default target, not a hard gate.** The replication supports a *continuous* comprehensibility score over a pass/fail threshold. Showing "you know 93% of this" is more honest than "comprehensible: yes/no".
- **Listening tolerates lower coverage (about 95%) than reading (about 98%).** Audio-first content can therefore sit slightly further above the learner.
- **Incidental learning needs many encounters, and repetition only correlates moderately.** So "just read and you'll pick it up" is slow for mid- and low-frequency words. That is the main empirical case for pairing input with deliberate review (§3).
- **Narrow input fits a node- or topic-based design well.** Several sentences on one scene or topic recycle the same content words, which raises coverage and repeat encounters together.
- **Hebrew (my inference).** Coverage figures are for English word families. In Hebrew the article and single-letter prepositions and conjunctions (ה, ב, ל, ו, מ, ש) attach to the word, and nouns take possessive suffixes. A naive whitespace token count will badly *under*-estimate coverage unless those clitics are stripped or the words lemmatised first.

### Gaps
- I found no coverage-by-frequency-band figures for **Hebrew** specifically. Nation's figures are for English, and Refold's "70% for any language" is unsourced.
- I did not get the effect sizes from the 2025 *Educational Psychology Review* meta-analysis.
- I found no strong experimental study showing narrow reading beats non-narrow reading on vocabulary *gains*, as opposed to corpus evidence of recycling.

## 3. The online "frequency list plus comprehensible input" method: what proponents say, and how well it matches research

### Takeaway
The typical online recipe runs in three steps:
1. Learn about the top 1,000-1,500 words from a frequency deck (Anki/SRS), plus basic grammar.
2. Switch to large amounts of input.
3. From then on, add vocabulary by "sentence mining" *i+1 / 1T* sentences (exactly one unknown item) from your own input into an SRS.

Dreaming Spanish sits at the pure end: no flashcards and delayed speaking. The research broadly supports the hybrid: deliberate learning of high-frequency words is efficient, and the top 1-2k words give a large share of coverage. It does *not* support precise thresholds ("switch at exactly 1,000"), the "any language" coverage figures, or long silent periods.

### Cited Findings
**Refold (claims)**
- **Stage structure.** Foundations, then comprehension through large amounts of input, then listening, then speaking, then accuracy and fluency. Speaking is scheduled after the comprehension and listening phases. — [Refold Roadmap](https://refold.la/roadmap/); [Refold CI library](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/comprehensible-input)
- **Foundation phase.** Estimated at 40-150 hours depending on the language. Claims the top 1,000 words are about 70% of any language. — [Refold 0C/Phase 1 overview](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-0/c/active-study/)
- **Frequency deck.** "Memorizing 1,500 words will give you a solid foundation". Use either a pre-made deck or a frequency list you turn into cards. — [Refold 1C: Vocabulary](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-1/c/vocabulary/) (from a search summary; the direct fetch returned only milestone text, so the exact wording is unconfirmed)
- **Sentence mining.** At Stage 2 you start a new deck of sentences mined from your own immersion. Sentence mining means finding sentences with unknown words, looking the words up, and saving them as cards. — [Refold Sentence Mining library](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/sentence-mining)
- **The rationale for the deck.** Learning a word creates a "mental dictionary entry" that primes you to notice it in immersion, and each later encounter adds to that entry. **This is effectively a lay version of Schmidt's noticing plus Uchihara's repetition findings, though Refold doesn't frame it that way.** — [Refold (via search summary)](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-0/c/active-study/)
- **Choosing content.** Refold's comprehensibility guide does *not* use known-word percentages. It uses "stacking supports":
  - visual context,
  - a familiar domain,
  - scripted speech,
  - moderate speed.

  You then remove supports as you improve. It cites Mayer's multimedia learning theory. — [Refold: Comprehensibility Factors](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/comprehensibility-factors)

**Dreaming Spanish / Dreaming (claims)**
- **"No drills, no memorization".** No flashcards, no grammar explanations, all input. Speaking "comes out naturally" later. Explicitly "developed from decades of research by … Dr. Stephen Krashen". — [Dreaming: How our method works](https://www.dreaming.com/method)
- **Roadmap v1.3 (January 2025).** The hour milestones are these:

  | Level | Hours of input | Known words (roadmap's estimate) | What the roadmap says you can do |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | 0 | about 0 | Starting from zero |
  | 2 | 50 | about 300 | Know some common words |
  | 3 | 150 | about 1,500 | Follow topics adapted for learners |
  | 4 | 300 | about 3,000 | Understand a patient native speaker |
  | 5 | 600 | not given | Understand people speaking normally |
  | 6 | 1,000 | not given | Comfortable with daily conversation |
  | 7 | 1,500 | not given | Effective user of the language |

  - It says to **multiply the hours by 2 for a language unrelated to yours** (its example is English to Arabic or Mandarin), and divide by 2 for a closely related one.
  - It says comprehension runs "1 or 2 levels above your production".
  - At Levels 1-2 it says speaking or writing is "not recommended yet". At Level 4 it suggests you "may want to wait a bit longer before speaking", depending on your tolerance for negative reactions.
  - It says reading is "not recommended" early "if you care about … pronunciation".

  — [Dreaming Spanish Language Learning Roadmap PDF v1.3](https://d3usdtf030spqd.cloudfront.net/Language_Learning_Roadmap_by_Dreaming_Spanish.pdf). **These are estimates from the company. The PDF itself says "general estimations".**

**Sentence mining and i+1 cards (community claims and tools)**
- **Migaku's definition.** A mined sentence is a card built from an "i+1 moment" in real media: the front is a sentence with one unknown word, the back has the meaning, audio and often a screenshot. — [Migaku: Sentence Mining Guide](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/sentence-mining-guide-learn-vocabulary-faster)
- **MorphMan (Anki add-on).** It tracks the words (morphs) you know and reorders cards so you only see sentences with **exactly one unknown word**. That word goes in a "Focus morph" field. One user reportedly organised a 50,000-card subs2srs sentence bank so that they understood 95% of sentences. **This is an anecdote.** — [AnkiWeb: MorphMan for Anki 2.1](https://ankiweb.net/shared/info/900801631); [GitHub kaegi/MorphMan](https://github.com/kaegi/MorphMan)
- **1T-Sentence-Miner.** An open-source tool that scans a text for sentences with exactly one unknown term (1T), scrapes definitions, and makes Anki cards automatically. — [GitHub amiothenes/1T-Sentence-Miner](https://github.com/amiothenes/1T-Sentence-Miner)

**Research on the deliberate-vocabulary half**
- **Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020)**, a meta-analysis in the *Modern Language Journal*: 22 studies and 100 effect sizes.
  - Intentional word-focused activities produced average gains of **60.1% on meaning recall** and **58.5% on form recall** on immediate posttests.
  - **Flashcards and word lists gave relatively large gains.** Writing and fill-in-the-blanks gave relatively small ones.
  - Gains ranged from 18.4-77.0% on immediate posttests and 23.9-73.4% on delayed posttests.

  — [Wiley MLJ abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12671); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/How-Effective-Are-Intentional-Vocabulary%E2%80%90Learning-A-Webb-Yanagisawa/7ed303720cba3acd650b0300e1f7257a60d5e725)
- **Incidental learning is slower per word.** Its relationship with repetition is only medium (r = .34). — [Uchihara et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343). See also the [Cambridge Language Teaching meta-analysis on incidental vocabulary learning](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-effective-is-second-language-incidental-vocabulary-learning-a-metaanalysis/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C); **I did not extract its figures.**
- **Word cards synthesis (2022).** A Frontiers research synthesis, "Learning English vocabulary from word cards", supports word cards as an efficient deliberate method. — [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.984211/full)

**How the popular claims line up with the research**

| Popular claim | What the research says |
|---|---|
| "Learn the top 1,000-2,000 words first" | Supported in spirit. The top 1-2k families cover about 72-85% of English text, and deliberate flashcard learning is efficient. But that coverage is well short of the 95-98% range, so a learner at 1,500 words still needs graded or adapted input, not native content. |
| "1,000 words is 70% of any language" | Roughly right for English. "Any language" is unsourced, and it depends on how "word" is counted, which matters for Hebrew's attached clitics. |
| "Understand 70-90% and context fills the rest" (Refold) | Refold's percentage is of *meaning understood*, not *tokens known*. Research coverage figures are much higher (95-98% tokens known). The two numbers measure different things and should not be conflated. |
| "Don't speak for hundreds of hours" (Dreaming Spanish) | Not supported as a necessity. Swain and Long both argue output and interaction contribute. There is no controlled evidence that early output harms adults. The worry about pronunciation fossilising from early speaking or reading is a community belief, not a research finding. |
| "i+1 = exactly one unknown word per sentence" | A practical community operationalisation of Krashen. Krashen's i+1 was about *structures*, not unknown-word counts. It lines up reasonably with the idea that learners infer and retain words better when the surrounding context is known. I found no direct experimental test of "exactly one unknown" against "two unknown" sentence cards. |

### Inferences
- The best-supported hybrid runs in three steps:
  1. Frequency-ordered deliberate learning of core vocabulary with SRS, where flashcards are among the most efficient deliberate methods.
  2. Early, heavily supported CI.
  3. Mined i+1 sentences to carry the long tail, since incidental learning alone is slow and only moderately driven by repetition.

  That is roughly Refold's shape, minus its long delay before speaking.
- **Dreaming's "×2 for an unrelated language" matters for Hebrew.** Its level-3 milestone (150 h, about 1,500 words) would be closer to 300 h for an English speaker, if one accepts their heuristic at all.
- **The i+1 sentence card is a good fit for a sentence-based app.** Its strength is that the unknown word arrives with a known context and audio. That is the combination the incidental-learning moderators favour: engagement, visual and audio support, and spaced repeat encounters.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch primary video transcripts from Matt vs Japan, Steve Kaufmann or specific popular "top 1000 words then input" videos. The characterisation of "a typical video" rests on Refold and Dreaming's own published materials, not a survey of videos.
- Clozemaster and Language Reactor were not researched in depth. From background knowledge (not sourced): Clozemaster presents frequency-sorted cloze sentences, and Language Reactor adds dual subtitles, word highlighting and saved-word lists to Netflix and YouTube.
- There is no independent outcome data for Refold or Dreaming Spanish learners. Only community self-reports exist.

## 4. How apps estimate known words and rate how comprehensible a text is, and whether it helps

### Takeaway
The dominant pattern is a per-user word-status model: new, learning or known, with frequency ranking on top. It supports three things: a known-word or new-word percentage per text, i+1 sentence detection (exactly one unknown), and "next word to learn" ordering. It is plausible, and consistent with the coverage research, but **I found no controlled evidence that these specific features improve outcomes**. The coverage research itself suggests a continuous score, not a hard cut-off. Migaku publicly pulled one of its comprehensibility-percentage fields because its accuracy was poor.

### Cited Findings
- **LingQ.**
  - Every word in a lesson is colour-coded: **blue** for new (never seen or marked), **yellow** for saved "LingQs" still being learned, and **white/unhighlighted** for known.
  - When you finish a lesson, the remaining blue words can be bulk-marked Known.
  - Each lesson shows its count or percentage of **new words** before you open it, so you can judge difficulty.

  — [LingQ Review 2026 (LingQ blog)](https://www.lingq.com/blog/lingq-review/); [LingQ support: How do I create LingQs?](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/how-do-i-create-lingqs); [LingQ forum: blue/yellow indicators](https://www.lingq.com/en/forum/support-feedback-forum/blueyellow-words-indicator-don/)
- **LingQ users want a different number.** Forum users have asked for a "known words percentage" indicator instead of, or as well as, the new-word count. That suggests the new-word count on its own is not the metric learners find intuitive. — [LingQ forum: known words percentage indicator](https://forum.lingq.com/t/wouldnt-a-known-words-percentage-indicator-be-better/29221)
- **Migaku.**
  - It identifies grammar patterns, marks unknown vocabulary and calculates **page comprehension scores**.
  - Its changelog records that the "Known Word Percentage" field was **temporarily removed from the Short Story Generator** until "comprehension score accuracy for generated content is improved".

  — [Migaku changelog](https://migaku.com/blog/changelog); [Migaku review 2026](https://wordy.info/blog/migaku-review)
- **MorphMan (Anki).** It keeps a database of known morphs (tokens or lemmas) from mature cards. It scores each card by its unknown morphs and schedules cards with **exactly one unknown** first, putting that word in a "Focus morph" field. It has separate parsers for Japanese and Chinese; "Language w/ Spaces" covers everything else. — [MorphMan21 README](https://github.com/ym1234/MorphMan21/blob/master/README.md); [kaegi/MorphMan](https://github.com/kaegi/MorphMan)
- **Dreaming Spanish** tracks progress by **total input hours**, broken down by difficulty level, rather than by known words. — [Dreaming progress page](https://app.dreaming.com/spanish/progress); [Roadmap PDF](https://d3usdtf030spqd.cloudfront.net/Language_Learning_Roadmap_by_Dreaming_Spanish.pdf)
- **Refold** deliberately avoids percentage metrics for choosing content and prefers qualitative factors: visuals, domain familiarity, speed, scripted versus unscripted. — [Refold: Comprehensibility Factors](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/comprehensibility-factors)
- **Research support for coverage-based scoring.** Coverage predicts comprehension (Hu & Nation 2000; Laufer 1989), but the relationship is linear, not a threshold (Kremmel et al. 2023). — [ERIC EJ887873](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887873.pdf); [Kremmel et al. 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12622)
- **The case for adaptive difficulty.** The 2025 Frontiers critique argues that adaptive systems which tune input and practice to each learner beat a fixed i+1. **This is an argument, not tested evidence.** — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12577063/)

### Inferences
- **Known-word models are only as good as the "known" signal.**
  - LingQ relies on self-marking, including bulk-marking every leftover blue word as known when a lesson ends. That will over-count known words.
  - MorphMan uses SRS maturity, which is stricter.
  - An app that checks production with speech recognition has a *stronger* signal than either: the learner actually produced the word correctly. It should weight that above passive exposure.
- **Report comprehensibility as a continuous coverage figure,** e.g. "~94% known", with a soft default target of about 95-98%. Don't gate content on a single threshold; Kremmel et al. 2023 found no cliff.
- **i+1 detection is cheap and robust** once the known-word model exists. Count the unknown lemmas per sentence and prefer sentences where that count is 1.
- **Hebrew-specific (my inference).** Tokenisation is the hard part. MorphMan's "Language w/ Spaces" mode would treat בבית, הבית and לבית as three separate "words". A Hebrew known-word model should key on the base word with attached ה/ב/ל/ו/מ/ש/כ stripped (and ideally the lemma), or its coverage and i+1 counts will be systematically pessimistic.
- **"Next word to learn" ordering.** Rank unknown words by frequency *weighted by how many near-i+1 sentences they would unlock*. That combines the frequency-deck logic with the sentence-mining logic. This is a design inference; no source tests it.

### Gaps
- I found **no controlled study** of whether LingQ-style highlighting, known-word percentages or i+1 ordering improve learning outcomes. The evidence is indirect: the coverage research, the noticing theory, and the repetition meta-analysis.
- I don't know how Migaku computes its score. It is not public in what I found.
- I did not check whether any app publishes a validated mapping from known-word percentage to comprehension for a language other than English.

## 5. Where output and speaking practice fit alongside CI, for a learner who mostly speaks sentences aloud and is checked by speech recognition

### Takeaway
Mainstream SLA gives output a real role. Pushed output helps learners notice gaps and build accuracy (Swain). Interaction and negotiation of meaning drive development (Long). Output is one of Nation's four equal strands. Speech-recognition feedback on pronunciation has a medium overall effect (g = 0.69), large with explicit corrective feedback (g = 0.86) and on individual sounds, and small on prosody. So a speak-aloud, recognition-checked practice loop is well supported *as one strand*. The main risk is too little meaning-focused input and too little genuine meaning-focused output, i.e. building sentences to say something rather than repeating given ones.

### Cited Findings
- **Swain** says input alone is insufficient: pushed output makes learners notice gaps and test hypotheses, and supports accuracy and fluency. — [JALT review of Swain](https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2198-output-and-beyond-dialogue-review-merrill-swains-current-approach-sla); [ERIC: An Empirical Study of the Role of Output](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1081989.pdf); a critique of Swain is at [ResearchGate: A Critique of Merrill Swain's Output Hypothesis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375608465_A_Critique_of_Merrill_Swain's_Output_Hypothesis_in_Language_Learning_and_Teaching)
- **Long:** interactional feedback (clarification requests, confirmation and comprehension checks) drives acquisition. — [Cambridge Language Teaching: Interaction and instructed SLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/interaction-and-instructed-second-language-acquisition/78A156EE200F744F5978F99BFB073DBE)
- **Nation** treats meaning-focused output and fluency development as two of four equal strands. Fluency work uses only familiar language, at speed. — [Nation, The Four Strands](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2167/illt039.0)
- **Ngo, Chen & Lai (ReCALL) meta-analysis of ASR for L2 pronunciation.** 15 studies, 38 effect sizes, 2008-2021.
  - **Overall g = 0.69.** With explicit corrective feedback, g = 0.86 across eight studies. Indirect feedback, such as dictation-style "did it transcribe correctly", was only moderately effective.
  - **Large effect on segmentals, small on suprasegmentals** (prosody and intonation).
  - **Short treatments showed no advantage;** medium or long durations did.
  - **Practising with peers gave a large effect; practising alone gave a small one.**
  - The strongest effects were for adult and intermediate learners.

  — [ReCALL (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D); [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronu/)
- **A mixed-methods study** found speech-recognition training improved EFL learners' pronunciation and speaking skills. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1210187/full)
- **The CI side's position.** Dreaming Spanish advises against speaking or writing at Levels 1-2 and suggests possibly waiting past Level 4 (300 h). It expects production to lag comprehension by 1-2 levels. — [Dreaming Roadmap PDF](https://d3usdtf030spqd.cloudfront.net/Language_Learning_Roadmap_by_Dreaming_Spanish.pdf). Refold schedules speaking after its comprehension and listening phases. — [Refold Roadmap](https://refold.la/roadmap/)
- **Explicit plus mixed modes.** In the updated Norris & Ortega meta-analysis, explicit instruction beat implicit, and combined oral-plus-written treatment worked best. — [Goo et al. 2015](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo)

### Inferences
- **The speak-aloud loop covers language-focused learning and fluency development well.** Speaking sentences aloud and being checked by speech recognition is also partly output. It is closer to *controlled* output (reproducing given sentences) than to Swain's *pushed* output (building your own message). Adding occasional prompts where the learner must produce a sentence for a meaning, rather than read one, would move part of it into genuine meaning-focused output.
- **The ASR meta-analysis supports three concrete design choices:**
  1. **Explicit corrective feedback beats "it transcribed / it didn't".** Show *which* word or sound failed, not just pass or fail.
  2. **Don't expect prosody gains from recognition checks.** Pair them with listening to and shadowing native audio for intonation.
  3. **Effects need sustained use.** Short trials show nothing, which argues for a daily habit loop over bursty sessions.
- **The "practising alone gave a small effect" finding** suggests adding a human element where possible, e.g. periodic checks with a native speaker. In this project that is George's girlfriend's spot-checks.
- **The main gap relative to CI is volume of meaning-focused input.** A sentence-drill app mostly delivers short, decontextualised sentences. Adding narrow, connected input (short linked passages or dialogues on the node's topic, heard with audio and pitched at about 95-98% coverage) would add the strand Krashen, Nation and the extensive-reading meta-analyses all agree matters. It would also give the repeated encounters that incidental learning needs.
- **Community advice to delay speaking to protect pronunciation is not research-backed.** For a learner already speaking with recognition feedback, there is no evidence-based reason to stop. The better-supported move is to make sure listening input comes before or alongside production of each new item, so there is a target sound to aim at.

### Gaps
- I found no study on **Hebrew** learners and speech-recognition feedback, or on recognition accuracy for non-native Hebrew. Performance on accented speech varies widely by language and engine.
- I found no study that directly compares input-only, input plus early output, and input plus delayed output in adult self-study.
- The ASR meta-analysis covers English learners (ESL/EFL) only. Transfer to Hebrew is an assumption.
